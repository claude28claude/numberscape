    1 Misperception

    Nobody knows
    where they live.

    Pollsters have spent a decade asking people to estimate basic facts about their own countries. The answers are not slightly off — they are off by two, five, sometimes twelve times. The amber dot is the average guess. The teal dot is reality. Look at which direction the errors run.

    What people guessed The actual figure

    The pattern is not random ignorance. We inflate whatever we are frightened of or angry about, and deflate whatever is quietly going right. The last row is the tell: the one question where the world is doing far better than people believe is the one where the error flips direction.

    Survey averages from Ipsos "Perils of Perception" (2014–2018), Gallup (2015–2022) and Gapminder's global public-knowledge surveys. The actual figure is the true value for the year each survey was run, so a row may differ slightly from today's number. Guesses are the mean of respondents' estimates — medians are less extreme but run the same way. These are national samples, not a claim about any individual.

    4 Survival

    Forty was not a lifespan.
    It was an average with
    fifteen dead babies in it.

    Life expectancy at birth in England and Wales was 41 in 1841 — the most quoted number in popular history and the most consistently misread. It is a mean taken over a population in which fifteen of every hundred newborns died before their first birthday and twenty-six before their fifth. Strip those deaths out and the same table says a five-year-old could expect fifty more years. Drag the scrubber and watch two hundred people die.

    Born 1841 — England & Wales
    100 of 100 alive
    Born today — same country
    100 of 100 alive
    0 yrs

    The other half of the correction. This is how many more years you could still expect, plotted against the age you had already reached. At birth the two eras are forty years apart. By sixty-five they are eight.

    Derived from ONS mortality rates (qx) by single year of age, England and Wales 1841–2016 (ad hoc release 008538), read against the published English Life Tables. The 1841 row is approximate: births were under-registered before the 1874 Act, which biases early-Victorian infant mortality downward, so the amber row is if anything too healthy. The 1841 remaining-life figures are period figures — they assume mortality never improves, which it promptly did, so a real 1841 five-year-old fared better than the 49.6 years shown. Two claims often attached to this chart are not supported by the table and are omitted: that forty was an unlikely age to die (it ranked 52nd of 125 single ages — squarely mid-pack; the least likely age was about twelve), and that the second commonest age of death was seventy-five (it was one). Seventy-five was the commonest adult age of death.

    6 Value

    Not what it costs.
    How much of your life
    it costs.

    "Everything is more expensive than it used to be" is stated as flatly obvious. Measured the only way that really matters — how many hours you must work to buy the thing — most of it collapsed to a rounding error. Your anxiety is not wrong. It is attached to the wrong objects.

    bar length = hours of work · dotted line = the earlier year

    Work time = price divided by the average hourly wage of production and nonsupervisory workers in the same year — the standard method from the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas 1997 annual report, Time Well Spent (Cox & Alm), which is also the source of every goods figure here. Service figures are calculated the same way from published prices: College Board Trends in College Pricing for tuition, the Census Housing Vacancy Survey for median asking rent, Child Care Aware of America for childcare. Wage anchors: $13.02/hour in early 1997 and $32.40/hour in July 2026 (BLS, production and nonsupervisory, total private). The Dallas Fed used manufacturing wages, which back-compute from their own 1997 figures to roughly $13.10 — close enough to splice, and the report itself notes the gap between the two series had all but closed by then.

    Four honest problems with this chart. Rows use different pairs of years, because each product is measured from when its price is documented; every bar is labelled with its own. Quality is not held constant — a 1954 colour television and a modern one are not the same object, and the report concedes this in its own first footnote; the comparison flatters the present. Wages here are pre-tax and exclude benefits, and both moved: taxes rose from about 5% of wages in the early 1950s to 21%, while non-wage benefits rose from 19% to 44%. Tuition is the published sticker price, not what families actually pay after aid — net tuition is far lower and has recently been falling, so the education bars overstate the burden on the average student while accurately describing the list price. All figures are United States.

    9 Immunity

    The infection that erases
    what you already survived.

    Measles is filed in most heads as a rash and a fortnight off school. A stronger version of the belief holds that having had it leaves a child hardier than a vaccinated one. The truth is precisely inverted. In 2019 two teams studied unvaccinated Dutch children before and after a measles outbreak — one profiling their antibodies against thousands of pathogen fragments, the other sequencing their B cells — and found the virus had deleted a large share of the immune memory those children had already built against everything else they had ever met.

    One child's antibody memory, sixty fragments of it. Choose an outcome:

    0% lostshare of antibody repertoire destroyed80% lost

    That is the mechanism. This is the consequence, and it is the older and larger finding: for years after an outbreak, children kept dying of other infections at elevated rates. Fitting historical mortality against past measles incidence gives the length of the shadow the virus casts:

    Repertoire loss: Mina et al., Science 366 (2019) — VirScan antibody profiling of 77 unvaccinated Dutch children roughly two months after infection; Petrova et al., Science Immunology 4 (2019) — B-cell receptor sequencing in a subset of the same cohort at about forty days. Two teams, two different methods, converging results. The macaque figure is an average across four animals, which is why it is drawn as a band and not a point. Shadow durations: Mina et al., Science 348 (2015), fitted lags from England & Wales, the United States and Denmark. An honest caution about that 2015 result: the raw mortality curves do not show non-measles deaths visibly falling in step behind measles deaths — non-measles infectious mortality was declining throughout the period anyway, on antibiotics, nutrition and sanitation. The finding is in the fitted model, which uses past measles incidence as a predictor with calendar year as a covariate, not in the shape of the lines. The fitted durations also scatter by disease, from about three months for septicaemia to about thirty for others.

    10 Dominance

    Weigh every
    mammal alive.

    Not count them — weigh them. Put every mammal on Earth on one enormous scale and sort the mass into three piles: livestock, human beings, and every wild mammal in existence — every elephant, whale, bat, mouse, deer, tiger and seal. Here is how that divides. Each square is one per cent.

    Livestock — cattle, pigs, sheep, goats Human beings All wild mammals on Earth

    Birds tell the same story: roughly 70% of all bird mass on the planet is poultry. Chickens outweigh every wild bird species combined, twice over.

    And yet — all life on Earth, by mass of carbon

    Animals are a rounding error. Plants are 82% of all living matter; everything that moves, from krill to blue whales, is under half a per cent of it. Humans are about 0.01% of life on Earth — and in becoming that 0.01% we have removed an estimated 83% of wild mammal biomass and half of all plant matter.

    Bar-On, Phillips & Milo, "The biomass distribution on Earth", PNAS (2018), measured in gigatonnes of carbon. The wild-mammal and plant loss figures are that paper's comparison against pre-human baselines and carry wide error bars — the ordering is solid, the exact percentages are not.

    11 Footprint

    Half the habitable
    land is a farm.

    Zoom in one step at a time. Start with the whole planet's surface, then keep only the part you could stand on, then only the part we farm. Each bar is the highlighted slice of the bar above it, stretched back out to full width. Watch what happens at the last step.

    Livestock's share of farmland, against what it puts on the table

    Grazing and animal feed occupy 77% of all farmland — an area roughly the size of North and South America combined — and return 18% of the world's calories. That is not an argument for or against eating meat. It is simply the shape of the land, and almost nobody carries an accurate picture of it in their head.

    Our World in Data, from FAO and Ellis et al. Land is 29% of Earth's surface; 71% of that land is habitable once glaciers and barren desert, salt flat and exposed rock are removed. "Livestock" covers both grazing land and cropland grown for animal feed. Calorie and protein shares are of global supply.

    12 Ageing

    More people.
    Not more children.

    Almost everyone assumes a growing world population means a growing number of children, and that the growth is driven by birth rates in poor countries. Neither is true any more. Annual births peaked around 2012 and have been falling since; the number of under-fifteens peaked in 2020 and is now shrinking. The world is still adding people, but it has stopped adding babies.

    So where did the extra billions come from? Decomposing world population growth from 1950 to 2026 by the age group that supplied it:

    Working age, 15–64 — 67.5% Children under 15 — 19.5% Aged 65+ — 13.0%

    UN World Population Prospects 2024, medium variant. Two of these peaks are thinner than they look and should not be quoted to the year: the 2012 births maximum and the 2017 under-five maximum are each within a fraction of a percent of neighbouring years, well inside the revision noise between WPP editions. The under-fifteen peak of 2020 is more robust. Projections beyond about 2030 are shown dashed because they are assumptions about fertility, not measurements — the UN's own 95% interval for annual births in 2100 runs from roughly 85 to 155 million, which is nearly the entire vertical range of this chart.

    15 Duration

    The thing you used
    for four minutes.

    Roughly how long common rubbish takes to break down, on a logarithmic scale — so every step right is ten times longer. The plastic bottle you drank from at lunch will outlast every person currently alive, and their grandchildren, and their language.

    Estimates vary enormously with conditions — sunlight, oxygen, temperature and burial depth change these numbers by orders of magnitude, and "biodegrades" often means "fragments into microplastic" rather than "disappears". Glass is the honest outlier: it does not meaningfully biodegrade at all, and the million-year figure is a placeholder for "indefinitely". Sources: NOAA, US National Park Service, WWF marine debris literature.

    16 Deep time

    Everything, in
    a single day.

    Compress the 4.54 billion years of Earth into twenty-four hours. Midnight is the planet forming out of dust; midnight again is right now, this second, you reading this. On that scale one second is 52,500 years — comfortably longer than all of recorded history.

    Zoom into the final minute — the last 3.1 million years
    And into the final second — the last 52,546 years

    Dates are the mainstream scientific consensus as of 2025 and carry real uncertainty, especially the early ones: the first life is placed somewhere between 4.1 and 3.5 billion years ago, and the origin of Homo sapiens has been pushed back repeatedly. The 24-hour compression is exact arithmetic on those dates — 4.54 billion years divided into 86,400 seconds is 52,546 years per second.

    20 Reflex

    How fast are you,
    really?

    Five rounds. The panel turns red, then — at some point it decides — green. Click the instant it does. You are measuring the round trip: light into your eye, signal to your visual cortex, decision, command back down your arm. Nobody is as fast as they think.

    Click to begin
    Five rounds — wait for green, then click fast
    — ms — your median

    Benchmarks: simple visual reaction time has a population median around 270 ms (Human Benchmark aggregate, millions of trials). World Athletics treats any sprint start under 100 ms as a false start on the grounds that no human can genuinely react that fast. Age and alcohol figures are approximate averages from published driving-simulator studies — individuals vary enormously. Your own score here also includes your mouse, your screen's refresh rate and your browser, so treat it as indicative, not clinical.

    21 Perception

    How strong is
    that relationship?

    Every dot is an observation. Estimate the correlation — 0 is a shapeless cloud, 1 is a perfect straight line — then see the real figure. Five rounds. There is a well-documented direction to the error people make here, and it matters, because this is the judgement call behind most "X is linked to Y" headlines.

    0.50
    0 · no relationship1 · perfect
    Round 1 of 5.

    Each cloud is 90 points drawn from a bivariate normal distribution with a known target correlation; you are scored against the actual sample correlation of the points on screen, computed after they are drawn, not the target. People reliably underestimate correlation from scatter plots — the perceived strength grows roughly with r², not r, so a genuinely strong r = 0.7 looks like a vague smear.

    23 Defence

    Twenty ways to be
    wrong, persuasively.

    Every chapter above can be misused. This is the toolkit for spotting it — including in the charts on this page. Filter by how the argument breaks, not by what it's about. The most useful ones are the boring ones: survivorship bias and the sunk cost fallacy will cost you more this year than every conspiracy theory combined.

    Named after David McCandless's "Rhetological Fallacies" poster, which merges rhetorical and logical fallacies into one taxonomy. Examples are written to be recognisable rather than fair to any particular side.
